The Season-Specific Packing Checklist

Alaska in summer isn't warm the way summer is warm elsewhere — you can be rained on all day in Ketchikan, chilled on a glacier in July, and sunburned at eleven at night, sometimes on the same trip. Spring and fall push the same logic further. The rule underneath all three seasons: layers over bulk.

The rule underneath every season

A base layer of wool or synthetic — never cotton, which holds cold water against you — then an insulating mid-layer like fleece or a light down, then a genuinely waterproof shell. This system lets you add and shed as the day swings from a cold ferry deck to a warm afternoon trail to a chilly glacier viewpoint, which it will. One big coat cannot do what three adjustable layers do.

On the coast, rain is the baseline, not the exception, in every season: bring a shell that genuinely keeps water out, not a water-resistant jacket that soaks through in an hour, and waterproof footwear for wet trails. A cheap poncho over a good base layer beats an expensive coat that wets out.

Late spring — roughly May to early June

The working range runs colder than peak summer, especially mornings, evenings, and anywhere near ice, altitude, or open water — pack for the cold end of "forties and fifties" and add a warmer layer on top of the base system above.

  • Wool/synthetic base layers (top and bottom) — no cotton
  • A warmer mid-layer than you think you need: fleece and a packable light-down jacket
  • A genuinely waterproof shell (not water-resistant)
  • Waterproof boots, broken in before the trip
  • Warm hat and gloves — mornings and glacier viewpoints run cold this early
  • Eye mask (blackout for the early long-daylight nights)
  • Binoculars
  • Sunscreen and sunglasses (the light reflects hard off snow and water even when the air is cool)
  • Insect repellent (building toward peak by late spring in the Interior)
  • A dry layer set aside just for camp/lodge use, so you always have something dry to change into

Summer — roughly mid-June to August

The working range is forties and fifties on the coast even at peak summer, warmer in the Interior, colder near ice or altitude. This is the core packing list Chapter 22 is built around.

  • Wool/synthetic base layers — no cotton
  • Fleece or a light down mid-layer
  • A genuinely waterproof shell
  • Waterproof boots or footwear for wet trails
  • Eye mask and a plan for blackout — the midnight sun will wreck your sleep otherwise
  • Binoculars — so much wildlife is seen at distance
  • Sunscreen and sunglasses — long days, hard reflected light, real sunburn risk even when cool
  • Insect repellent — mosquitoes in the Interior and in high summer are legendary and relentless
  • Layers you can get wet without ruining, because you will

Fall — roughly September

The days shorten fast and temperatures drop, particularly in the Interior and at night; treat this as colder than late spring, and add real insulation. This is also aurora season, which rewards clear, dark, cold nights — dress for standing still outside for a while.

  • Wool/synthetic base layers, heavier weight than summer
  • A real insulating mid-layer (fleece plus down, not either/or)
  • A genuinely waterproof shell
  • Insulated, waterproof boots
  • Warm hat, gloves, and an extra layer for standing still (aurora viewing, evening wildlife watching)
  • Eye mask (still useful early in the month; less critical as nights lengthen)
  • Binoculars
  • Sunscreen and sunglasses — lower sun angle, still real glare off water
  • Insect repellent — usually easing off by September, but bring it
  • Extra warm bedding if you packed for summer and are extending into fall

The RV note, every season

Know your rig's heat source (propane or diesel) and how to run it — even summer nights get cold, especially in the Interior and the shoulder seasons. If you're traveling early or late in the season, ask the rental company about winterizing precautions and freezing risk for the water system. Carry more warm bedding than a summer instinct suggests: a cold night in a metal box on the tundra is colder than the forecast implies.

The mistake first-timers make here

Packing for the word "summer" instead of for Alaska. One light jacket, cotton everything, no rain shell — then cold, wet, and sunburned at the same time. Pack layers of wool or synthetic, a rain shell that truly works, waterproof boots, an eye mask, binoculars, sunscreen, and bug spray, and you're ready for the weather Alaska actually delivers.
